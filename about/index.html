About

I came to engineering through making things, and stayed for the machines.

I'm VP of Operations at Trener Robotics, where I deploy AI-enabled automation into the field, usually machine shops. I work mostly with industrial robots and cobots, and I've worked with quadrupeds and mobile robotic platforms along the way.

How I got here

Not in a straight line. I studied business administration at Cornell because I was interested in entrepreneurship, took an MFA at RISD in industrial design and the processes of manufacturing, and a master's in computer science at Georgia Tech. What connects them is that I'm a hands-on learner: theory doesn't stick for me until it's attached to a physical thing.

While I was studying how objects actually get made, I got obsessed with virtuosity: with becoming machine-like, and with what happens when you try to scale that. For me that meant glass. Years of glassblowing, casting, and cutting, working like a machine to get the hand right. Then I found out what CNC machines could do, and the obsession moved. That pushed me into machining processes and then into robotics, and what had been a design practice turned into an engineering one. Form follows function, and function is decided on the shop floor.

My grandparents owned Stanko Products, a machine shop founded in 1965, so the state of manufacturing in the US isn't abstract to me. Seeing that struggle up close is what convinced me automation was worth doing. Not to remove people from shops, but to keep those shops open.

Now

Physical AI and perception have moved fast in the last few years. I'm working out how to use that to put automation within reach of shops that could never afford a full integration. That's why I'm at Trener.
