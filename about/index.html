About

I came to engineering through making things, and stayed for the machines.

I'm VP of Operations at Trener Robotics, where I deploy AI-enabled automation into the field — usually machine shops. I work mostly with industrial robots and cobots, and I've worked with quadrupeds and mobile robotic platforms along the way.

How I got here

Not in a straight line. I studied business administration at Cornell because I was interested in entrepreneurship, took an MFA at RISD in design and the fabrication of objects, and I'm now finishing a master's in computer science at Georgia Tech. What connects them is that I'm a hands-on learner: theory doesn't stick for me until it's attached to a physical thing.

While I was studying how objects actually get made, I got obsessed with virtuosity — with becoming machine-like, and with what happens when you try to scale that. For me that meant glass: years of glassblowing, casting, and cutting, working like a machine to get the hand right. Then I found out what CNC could do, and the obsession moved. That pushed me into machining processes and then into robotics, and what had been a design practice turned into an engineering one. Form follows function, and function is decided on the shop floor.

My grandparents owned a machine shop, so the state of manufacturing in the US isn't abstract to me. Seeing that struggle up close is what convinced me automation was worth doing — not as a way to remove people from shops, but as a way to keep those shops viable.

Now

I'm working out how to leverage the recent advances in physical AI and perception to democratize automation — to get it into the hands of far more people than the ones who can already afford a full integration. That's the whole project, and it's why I'm at Trener.
